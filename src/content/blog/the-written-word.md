## Introduction
Speaking has never really been my thing. It takes a particular set of skills that I simply don’t have. I can’t guarantee how my words will sound when they leave my mouth, let alone how someone else will parse them. Ever since I was a child, I’ve relied on text to make my point and work out what other people are actually trying to say.

Writing is the most accessible API we have for interacting with the world. We can pass strange, abstract concepts to one another simply by scribbling symbols on a page. That is essentially D&D magic made real.

I like it enough that I thought the first post on this blog should explain why I bother. Writing is my job, my hobby and a massive pain.

## Writing for work
I work with ideas. I share them; I consume them. I write things down so that people, and increasingly machines, can compile what I’m thinking and do something useful with it. I’m a mumbler in real life because my brain runs far faster than my mouth can keep up with. Typing forces me to throttle down and format my thoughts into something usable.

It sounds odd, but putting an abstract concept into words makes it tangible. It sticks in my memory, and other people stand a chance of understanding what I’m on about. My work is heavily asynchronous, so progress depends on aligning thoughts rather than schedules. Putting mine into a chat thread gives everyone something concrete to return to, instead of letting the conversation drift awkwardly into the void.

## Writing for myself
Off the clock, writing is entirely mine. There are no performance metrics, no sprints and no deadlines. It’s just me messing around with words until they click. It’s a quiet thing to do, but it makes me feel far more connected to the rest of the world. I can build entire systems without provisioning servers, managing dependencies or worrying about taking down production.

## Writing through the pain
It is relentlessly hard. The blank page is miserable, and the gap between the brilliant idea in my head and the rubbish first draft on the screen is a pit I fall into daily. I can spend hours fighting with a single paragraph, deleting most of it and wondering why I do this to myself.

But I keep doing it. The friction makes the result better and, eventually, the words stay put. Even now, when a machine can produce a clean paragraph in seconds, enduring the awful first draft still feels like a necessary tax: the price of pinning down a fleeting thought before it disappears entirely.
