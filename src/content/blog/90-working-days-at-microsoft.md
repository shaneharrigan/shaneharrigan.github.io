## Introduction

I have now worked at Microsoft for 90 working days. That is weekdays rather than calendar days, because apparently I am willing to mark a career milestone but not willing to give Microsoft credit for my weekends.

Before joining, I worried that I would disappear.

I was used to smaller ponds: places where it was relatively easy to understand how the pieces fitted together and where my work landed. Microsoft is not a pond. It is an ocean with its own weather systems, shipping lanes and several perfectly reasonable definitions of the word "tenant." I imagined myself floating around inside it, trying to work out where I belonged.

I also expected everyone to be impossibly clever. Microsoft has spent decades hiring the kind of engineers whose work becomes the foundation beneath somebody else's foundation. I wondered whether joining them would finally expose me as a fraud who had wandered through several successful interviews by administrative accident.

Ninety working days later, I have not mastered the ocean. I have learned that this was never the job. The surprise has been how quickly a place this large can become human, how its scale can connect people rather than isolate them and how rapidly one engineer can still make an impact.

## The First System Was a Person

Large organisations need onboarding systems. There are accounts to create, policies to read, tools to install and enough acronyms to make ordinary language feel like a legacy dependency.

The best part of my onboarding was not a system at all. It was my buddy.

Microsoft's onboarding paired me with someone who could answer the questions that were too small, too specific or too embarrassing for a formal document. More importantly, I met an awesome person who made the organisation feel navigable before I understood how to navigate it.

That experience told me something early: a people-first culture does not mean removing process. At this scale, process is unavoidable. It means putting a person beside the process so that a new employee does not have to compile the organisation alone.

I had expected to spend my first weeks proving that I deserved to be there. Instead, somebody was already invested in helping me succeed. That is a subtle difference, but it changes the question from "How do I avoid looking lost?" to "Who can help me find the right path?"

The second question is much more useful. It also has much better answers.

## Scale Can Make People Easier to Reach

I assumed that a bigger company would mean more distance between me and the person who knew the thing I needed to understand. Sometimes it does. There are more teams, more layers and more context than any one person can hold.

The surprising part is how connected that context can be.

The value is not simply that Microsoft has a lot of information. Every sufficiently old organisation has a lot of information; sometimes it is even stored somewhere people can find it. The value is access to people and expertise. A question can lead to a document, which leads to a discussion, which leads to the person who designed the system or understands why it behaves the way it does.

AI is part of that connection, but not in the way the loudest predictions describe it. My experience has been AI enhancing person-to-person work rather than replacing it. It can help locate context, condense the distance between a question and the right starting point, or make it easier to arrive at a conversation prepared.

The destination is still often another human.

That matters because expertise is more than information retrieval. The useful answer may depend on history, trade-offs, customer needs or the reason an apparently obvious approach was tried three years ago and set a small metaphorical fire. AI can help us reach that context faster. The judgement still emerges from people working together.

At Microsoft's scale, no one knows everything. The more valuable skill is learning how to connect what you know with what somebody else knows. The company feels less like one enormous brain and more like a very large network: useful not because every node contains the whole system, but because the nodes can find one another.

## The Technology Does Not Wait Politely

The other surprise has been the pace.

It is easy to think of a company this large as slow by necessity. There are good reasons for caution when software reaches billions of people and businesses. A mistake does not remain a charming local incident for long.

At the same time, Microsoft Copilot is being integrated through layers of Windows and Azure at lightning speed. The technology changes, the products respond and the assumptions underneath both are continuously revised. There is no comfortable point at which everybody agrees that AI is now finished and we can move on to maintaining it.

Working in that environment feels like helping to build the aircraft while the field of aeronautics is being updated.

What I did not expect was how quickly I could contribute to it myself. Scale has not removed individual ownership. I can make a change, follow it into the features I own and then encounter those features during my own working day. My work does not vanish into Microsoft. It lives there.

That has been one of the most satisfying adjustments. In a huge organisation, impact is not always one dramatic launch with your name attached. Sometimes it is recognising your decisions in a product, seeing the behaviour you helped shape and knowing that your piece has joined a much larger system.

The feedback loop is unusually direct: I work on Copilot, and then Copilot appears in the Windows and Azure tools around my work. There are not many jobs where you can meet your own output this often without having accidentally introduced a recurring bug.

## Impostor Syndrome Is Not a Performance Review

I was right about one thing before joining: Microsoft engineers are extremely clever.

The mistake was treating their ability as evidence against my own.

There will always be somebody who knows more about a language, a system, a customer or a domain. At Microsoft, there may be an entire distribution list of them. That does not make my experience irrelevant. I arrived through an indirect route: open-source software, academia, neuromorphic computing, robotics and engineering across very different industries. None of that makes me the smartest person in every room. It does mean I sometimes see a problem from an angle the room needs.

I still feel unworthy of the role. Ninety working days have not produced the cinematic moment where the music rises and I finally announce that I deserve everything. I am not sure I want one.

That discomfort drives me to learn, but it needs limits. Used carefully, it keeps me curious and stops confidence hardening into certainty. Used badly, it converts every gap in my knowledge into a verdict on my value. One helps me become a better engineer. The other merely makes an engineer miserable.

The lesson has been to let the feeling make me better without letting it make me less human. I can ask the obvious question. I can admit that I do not know. I can rely on somebody else's expertise and offer mine when it becomes useful. Being surrounded by brilliant people is not an examination I am expected to pass alone. It is the main benefit of being here.

## What 90 Working Days Have Taught Me

I joined Microsoft worried that its size would make me small. Instead, its scale has made the importance of individual relationships much clearer.

A buddy turned onboarding from a process into a welcome. A connected culture made a huge body of expertise feel reachable. AI shortened the path between people rather than removing people from the path. Rapid changes across Windows, Azure and Copilot created room for me to contribute sooner than I thought possible.

Most importantly, I can feel my work living inside Microsoft. I interact with features I own. I see where my decisions meet a much larger product, and I know that the organisation is different in small but real ways because I joined it.

I am still learning the acronyms. I am still occasionally lost. I still work with people who make me wonder whether they were issued additional processing cores at birth.

But I no longer feel as though I am floating around an ocean with nothing to hold on to. I have people, work that matters and a growing understanding of where I can be useful.

Ninety working days is not long enough to understand Microsoft. It has been long enough to understand why I want to keep trying.
